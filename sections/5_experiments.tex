\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We conduct experiments on CIFAR-100~\citep{krizhevsky2009learning} with ResNet18~\citep{he2016deep}. The class-rich label space provides a direct test of CARAT's class-balanced hidden tasks. We partition the training set among $20$ clients using a Dirichlet distribution with $\alpha\in\{1.0,0.5\}$, where the smaller concentration produces greater client heterogeneity. All clients participate in every round. Each run lasts $200$ communication rounds, and clients perform four local epochs of SGD per round with learning rate $0.05$, momentum $0.9$, weight decay $5\times10^{-4}$, and batch size $64$.

We evaluate four untargeted model-poisoning attacks: ALIE~\citep{baruch2019alie}, FangAttack~\citep{fang2020local}, and the MinMax and MinSum attacks~\citep{shejwalkar2021manipulating}. Malicious clients constitute $20\%$ of the federation, corresponding to four of the $20$ participants. We compare CARAT against unprotected Mean aggregation, NormClipping, MultiKrum~\citep{blanchard2017krum}, FLTrust~\citep{cao2021fltrust}, and FLDetector~\citep{zhang2022fldetector}. These baselines span direct averaging, norm control, geometric filtering, trusted-data anchoring, and historical anomaly detection.

CARAT uses eight hidden tasks, four probe examples per class and task, a $512$-example probe pool, and a probe batch size of $128$. We set the probe-radius quantile to $0.5$, the MAD clipping factor to $2.5$, the certificate temperature to $12$, the rank and temporal-prior weights to $0.20$ and $0.05$, and the attacker-fraction prior to the true experimental value $\hat{\rho}=0.2$. Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-experiments} provides the complete configuration and result provenance.

The new CARAT sweep uses five independent runs with seeds $42$--$46$. Compatible legacy logs provide five seeds for Mean, NormClipping, MultiKrum, and FLDetector and three seeds for FLTrust. Table~\ref{tab:cifar100-main-acc} reports final-round accuracy as the mean and sample standard deviation over the available seeds. Average rank is computed across the four attacks within each heterogeneity setting. The CARAT row is shaded for identification rather than as a claim of statistical significance. CARAT and FLTrust also use different server-reference budgets: CARAT draws a $512$-example pool and FLTrust uses $100$ examples. In both cases, these examples come from the same public training set used to form client partitions and are not removed from client data. We therefore interpret their rows as the performance of the recorded method--reference configurations, not as an isolated comparison of aggregation rules.

\subsection{Experimental Results}

\subsubsection{Mitigating Model-Poisoning Attacks}
Table~\ref{tab:cifar100-main-acc} shows that CARAT remains competitive across all four attacks and both levels of heterogeneity. It ranks second under ALIE and FangAttack and third under MinMax and MinSum, yielding an average rank of $2.50$ for both $\alpha$ values. Its mean accuracy is within $0.11$--$1.22$ percentage points of the best method in each of the eight attack--heterogeneity cells. Averaged across attacks, CARAT obtains $49.06\%$ for $\alpha=1.0$ and $47.56\%$ for $\alpha=0.5$, compared with $49.82\%$ and $48.23\%$ for Mean, the strongest method on average.

The relative behavior depends on the attack. Mean has the highest mean accuracy in six of the eight cells, while FLDetector leads narrowly under MinMax at $\alpha=1.0$ and MinSum at $\alpha=0.5$. FLDetector is much less stable under FangAttack, falling to $28.54\%$ and $27.77\%$. CARAT avoids such attack-specific collapse and exceeds MultiKrum and FLTrust in every displayed cell, by $0.90$--$14.07$ and $7.69$--$12.90$ percentage points, respectively. The comparison therefore supports CARAT as a consistent reference-assisted defense, but it does not show that CARAT uniformly outperforms simple averaging in this protocol.

\subsubsection{Impact of Client Heterogeneity}
Reducing the Dirichlet concentration from $1.0$ to $0.5$ lowers CARAT's four-attack mean by $1.51$ percentage points, from $49.06\%$ to $47.56\%$. This is the smallest decrease among the six displayed methods: the corresponding reductions are $1.59$ points for Mean, $1.74$ for NormClipping, $2.52$ for MultiKrum, $3.15$ for FLTrust, and $1.66$ for FLDetector. CARAT also retains the same average rank in both settings. These results indicate limited degradation over the two tested heterogeneity levels, although they do not establish robustness under more extreme partitions such as $\alpha=0.1$.

\subsubsection{Component Contributions}
We conduct a focused component study on CIFAR-100/ResNet18 with $20$ clients, $20\%$ malicious clients, and $\alpha=0.5$. Each variant uses the same five seeds as its within-study control. One task replaces $T=8$ with $T=1$; no clipping disables MAD clipping; no rank penalty sets its coefficient to zero; and no temporal prior removes the temporal regularizer. The ablation runs were executed independently from the main comparison, so the full CARAT row in Table~\ref{tab:carat-ablation} is the relevant control.

Full CARAT achieves the highest cross-attack mean, but the separation is modest: its advantage over the reduced variants ranges from $0.06$ to $0.28$ percentage points and is smaller than the seed-level variation in individual attack cells. Full CARAT leads under ALIE and FangAttack, whereas no clipping is highest under MinMax and the one-task variant is highest under MinSum. In particular, $T=1$ nearly matches $T=8$ in aggregate. The ablation therefore suggests a small combined benefit in this setting, rather than a large and monotonic contribution from every component.

\subsubsection{Runtime Overhead}
Figure~\ref{fig:verification-curves} reports a separate, earlier CIFAR-100/i.i.d.\ ALIE verification run with three seeds. CARAT records an average per-round time of $9.94$ seconds, compared with $2.80$ seconds for Mean under attack, or approximately $3.6\times$ higher wall-clock cost. This premium is consistent with CARAT evaluating every candidate update on repeated hidden tasks. The diagnostic predates the five-seed main sweep, so it quantifies the implementation tradeoff in that verification setting rather than providing hardware-normalized timing for Table~\ref{tab:cifar100-main-acc}.

\subsubsection{Scope of the Evidence}
The experiments cover one dataset--model pair, $20$ fully participating clients, two non-i.i.d.\ concentrations, a known attacker fraction used by CARAT's weight cap, and four untargeted attacks. They do not cover partial participation, larger federations, backdoor or data-poisoning attacks, misspecified attacker fractions, or adaptive attackers that infer the hidden tasks. The overlap between server-reference and client-training examples and the unequal CARAT/FLTrust reference budgets further limit causal attribution. Together with the strong Mean baseline and the small ablation gaps, these boundaries restrict the empirical claim to competitiveness in the reported setting rather than universal robustness.

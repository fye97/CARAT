\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
\subsection{Experimental Setup}
We evaluate CIFAR-10/VGG19~\citep{krizhevsky2009learning,simonyan2015verydeep} and CIFAR-100/ResNet18~\citep{he2016deep} with $20$ fully participating clients, Dirichlet non-i.i.d.\ partitions ($\alpha\in\{1.0,0.5,0.1\}$), $200$ communication rounds, and four local epochs per round. We test ALIE~\citep{baruch2019alie}, FangAttack~\citep{fang2020local}, MinMax, MinSum~\citep{shejwalkar2021manipulating}, and Mimic~\citep{karimireddy2022bucketing} with $10\%$ malicious clients, and compare CARAT with NormClipping, MultiKrum~\citep{blanchard2017krum}, FLTrust~\citep{cao2021fltrust}, and FLDetector~\citep{zhang2022fldetector}. Unattacked Mean is the clean reference. Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-experiments} gives the optimizer and method details.

Figures~\ref{fig:table1-curves-cifar100} and~\ref{fig:table1-curves-cifar10} report means and sample-standard-deviation bands over seeds $42$--$46$; numerical comparisons below use round 200. CARAT uses $T=8$ hidden tasks and a $512$-example server pool, whereas FLTrust uses $100$ server examples. Both pools are drawn from the training set without removing those examples from client partitions, so the curves compare the recorded method--reference configurations rather than isolated aggregation rules. With a held-out pool and budgets matched at $100$ and $512$ examples, CARAT's accuracy is unchanged and FLTrust does not improve (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-reference-control}). The shared NoAttack + Mean curve is excluded from defense rankings. After fixing non-finite-certificate handling that could propagate NaNs to all aggregation weights and the model, we reran $63$ affected runs with identical seeds and configurations: $3$ in the main comparison (Figures~\ref{fig:table1-curves-cifar100}--\ref{fig:table1-curves-cifar10}), $22$ in Table~\ref{tab:carat-client-scaling}, and $38$ further runs in Figure~\ref{fig:attacker-fraction}. Nine further runs in Table~\ref{tab:carat-client-scaling} whose originals had not completed were run for the first time with the fixed code. Runs with entirely finite certificates were retained because the fix leaves them unchanged; the uniform-weight fallback never fired.

\subsection{Experimental Results}

\subsubsection{Robustness to Model-Poisoning Attacks}
Figure~\ref{fig:table1-curves-cifar100} shows that CARAT closely tracks the clean trajectory after the learning-rate transitions, rather than reaching a similar endpoint through a late recovery. On CIFAR-100 with $\alpha\in\{1.0,0.5\}$, CARAT is the strongest attacked defense under FangAttack and is second under each of the other conventional attacks. In those six second-place cases it trails the strongest defense by only $0.18$--$0.54$ percentage points. It also avoids FLDetector's sharp FangAttack degradation and exceeds MultiKrum and FLTrust throughout both settings. Relative to the NoAttack + Mean references of $52.47\%$ and $51.61\%$, CARAT's conventional-attack results remain within $0.95$--$1.91$ points.

At $\alpha=0.1$, CARAT is the strongest attacked defense under FangAttack and MinMax, and is second under ALIE and MinSum by $0.29$ and $0.16$ points, respectively. Its four means remain within $1.14$--$2.85$ points of the $45.94\%$ clean reference, with seed-level standard deviations below one percentage point. This is evidence of stability for the evaluated $10\%$ threat level, not evidence that the same behavior persists as the malicious population grows.

On CIFAR-10 at $\alpha=1.0$, CARAT is the strongest attacked defense under FangAttack and is second under ALIE, MinMax, and MinSum, with gaps of $0.49$, $0.44$, and $0.53$ points. At $\alpha=0.5$, it leads all five attacks, including MinSum at $77.15\pm0.71\%$, a $0.37$-point lead over FLDetector. At $\alpha=0.1$, it again leads all five attacks, with MinMax and MinSum accuracies of $55.59\pm3.65\%$ and $53.63\pm4.32\%$, exceeding FLDetector by $5.02$ and $1.72$ points, respectively. No CARAT seed in the main comparison finishes at chance level.

Mimic copies a fixed benign update rather than optimizing an outlier. CARAT ranks second on CIFAR-100 at all three heterogeneity levels, trailing FLDetector by $0.46$--$1.08$ points. On CIFAR-10, it is second at $\alpha=1.0$ and leads at $\alpha\in\{0.5,0.1\}$ with $76.96\%$ and $53.96\%$. It exceeds NormClipping, MultiKrum, and FLTrust in every Mimic setting, indicating that hidden-task validation retains signal against copied benign updates.

\subsubsection{Impact of Client Heterogeneity}
On CIFAR-100, CARAT's accuracy averaged across all five attacks is $51.30\%$, $49.94\%$, and $44.05\%$ for $\alpha=1.0$, $0.5$, and $0.1$, respectively, trailing the corresponding clean references by $1.17$, $1.67$, and $1.89$ points. The $1.36$-point decline from $\alpha=1.0$ to $0.5$ grows to $5.89$ points from $0.5$ to $0.1$, but remains consistent across attacks. On CIFAR-10, the corresponding attack averages are $79.38\%$, $76.85\%$, and $54.34\%$, compared with clean references of $79.83\%$, $77.07\%$, and $53.53\%$. The cross-setting drops of $2.53$ and $22.51$ points show that the CIFAR-10 decline is concentrated at $\alpha=0.1$. Client heterogeneity therefore remains an important source of degradation even when the malicious-client fraction is $10\%$, with a substantially sharper effect on CIFAR-10/VGG19.

\subsubsection{Impact of Attacker Fraction}
We next vary the malicious-client fraction from $10\%$ to $40\%$ (two to eight of the same $20$ clients) while retaining the three Dirichlet settings and five attacks, and compare CARAT with the four baseline defenses and with undefended Mean averaging under attack. Figure~\ref{fig:attacker-fraction} averages the attack accuracies within each seed, then reports the mean and sample standard deviation across seeds at round 200. On CIFAR-100, the $10\%$-to-$40\%$ increase reduces CARAT's attack-averaged accuracy from $51.30\%$ to $42.17\%$ at $\alpha=1.0$, from $49.94\%$ to $38.86\%$ at $\alpha=0.5$, and from $44.05\%$ to $30.78\%$ at $\alpha=0.1$. CIFAR-10 shows the same endpoint trend, declining from $79.38\%$ to $66.86\%$, $76.85\%$ to $61.36\%$, and $54.34\%$ to $33.04\%$, respectively. The five-attack mean is non-increasing with the malicious fraction in every dataset and heterogeneity setting. CARAT has the highest attack-averaged accuracy among the defenses in $22$ of the $24$ dataset--heterogeneity--fraction settings. Its margin over the strongest baseline, usually FLDetector, is $2.81$--$5.13$ points at $10\%$ and $7.58$--$10.07$ points at $40\%$ outside CIFAR-10 at $\alpha=0.1$. The two exceptions are CIFAR-10 at $\alpha=0.1$ with $30\%$ and $40\%$ malicious clients, where FLTrust reaches $40.18\%$ and $34.40\%$ compared with CARAT's $35.37\%$ and $33.04\%$.

The undefended Mean reference shows that the evaluated attacks are weak at low malicious fractions. Averaged over the same five attacks, Mean and CARAT differ by at most $1.17$ points in $9$ of the $12$ settings at $10\%$ and $20\%$, and Mean is slightly ahead in $8$ of them; CARAT leads by more than one point only at $\alpha=0.1$ ($1.32$--$3.32$ points). At $40\%$, the same attacks reduce Mean substantially, and CARAT exceeds it by $5.55$--$7.71$ points on CIFAR-100 and by $13.82$, $10.48$, and $2.82$ points on CIFAR-10 at $\alpha=1.0$, $0.5$, and $0.1$. The accuracy gaps to the baselines at $10\%$ therefore mainly reflect the baselines' lower clean accuracy rather than stronger filtering by CARAT; CARAT's robustness advantage appears at $30$--$40\%$. Higher attacker fractions generally magnify the impact of heterogeneity, but the plot summarizes attack-specific outcomes and does not imply uniform degradation for every attack. CARAT's weight cap uses the specified attacker fraction, so this sweep changes both the malicious population and the supplied prior; it does not test prior misspecification.

Genuine robustness failures remain without NaNs: on CIFAR-10 at $\alpha=0.1$, ALIE and FangAttack reach only $18.85\%$ and $17.15\%$ at $30\%$ malicious clients, and $24.60\%$ and $17.00\%$ at $40\%$, approaching the $10\%$ chance level. At $40\%$ malicious clients, FangAttack reaches $41.10\%$ and $57.69\%$ on CIFAR-10 at $\alpha=0.5$ and $1.0$, while Mimic reaches $48.31\%$ and $28.33\%$ at $\alpha=1.0$ and $0.1$; on CIFAR-100 at $\alpha=0.1$, Mimic reaches $18.86\%$. These are limits of robustness rather than numerical failures.

\subsubsection{Scope of the Evidence}
Across the reruns, whenever certificates were non-finite, the flagged clients were exactly the full malicious set and never included a benign client; this occurred mainly under FangAttack, in $8$--$87$ of $200$ rounds in FangAttack runs where the rule fired. This part of the observed FangAttack robustness therefore comes from rejecting non-finite certificates, which an adaptive attacker could avoid.

The main comparison does not cover partial participation, backdoor or data-poisoning attacks, misspecified attacker fractions, or adaptive attackers that infer the hidden tasks. The NoAttack + Mean curve is a clean reference rather than an attacked defense. The reference-data control covers only $\alpha=0.5$, and at $10\%$ malicious clients CARAT down-weights only FangAttack clients; the near-mean attacks keep the capped weight (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-reference-control}). Zeno, a simpler loss-based rule, is compared only at $10\%$ malicious clients, and the evidence at $30$--$40\%$ concerns CARAT as a whole rather than the separate contributions of its components or of the non-finite-certificate rule. Figure~\ref{fig:attacker-fraction} compares the baselines at higher attacker fractions only with $20$ clients. The separate component study, client-population study, and earlier three-seed runtime diagnostic are reported in Appendices~\ref{sec:appendix-component-study}, \ref{sec:appendix-scalability}, and~\ref{sec:appendix-runtime}, respectively. Together with the genuine failures at higher malicious-client fractions, these boundaries restrict the claim to the evaluated settings.

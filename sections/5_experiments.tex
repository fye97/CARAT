\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate a deliberately scoped question: whether hidden-task certificates provide a competitive aggregation signal under heterogeneous client data and untargeted model poisoning. The main study uses CIFAR-100/ResNet18, four attacks, two non-i.i.d.\ settings, and a five-seed rerun of CARAT. Because the available baseline logs and the new CARAT runs differ in seed count and server-reference construction, we report those differences explicitly rather than treating the comparison as a fully controlled head-to-head experiment. Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-experiments} records the detailed protocol, raw-loss results for CARAT, and result provenance.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}

\paragraph{Benchmark and threat model.}
We use CIFAR-100~\citep{krizhevsky2009learning} with ResNet18~\citep{he2016deep}. Its class-rich label space is useful for testing whether class-balanced hidden tasks reveal semantic degradation that may be obscured by update geometry. The attacked comparison covers four untargeted model-poisoning attacks: ALIE~\citep{baruch2019alie}, FangAttack~\citep{fang2020local}, MinMax, and MinSum~\citep{shejwalkar2021manipulating}. Backdoor and data-poisoning attacks remain outside the empirical claim set.

\paragraph{Baselines and reference data.}
The comparison includes Mean, NormClipping, MultiKrum~\citep{blanchard2017krum}, FLTrust~\citep{cao2021fltrust}, FLDetector~\citep{zhang2022fldetector}, and CARAT. This set spans unprotected averaging, clipping, geometry-based filtering, trusted-data anchoring, historical detection, and hidden-task validation. CARAT constructs a $512$-example probe pool, whereas the recorded FLTrust configuration uses $100$ server examples; these budgets are not matched. Moreover, the server examples are sampled from the same public CIFAR-100 training set used to construct client partitions and are not removed from client data. Consequently, comparisons involving reference-assisted defenses should be read as comparisons between the recorded method--reference configurations, not as an isolation of the aggregation rule alone.

\paragraph{Training and reporting protocol.}
All attacked runs use $20$ clients with full participation, $200$ global rounds, $4$ local epochs, learning rate $0.05$, and a $20\%$ malicious-client ratio. We evaluate Dirichlet non-i.i.d.\ regimes with $\alpha\in\{1,0.5\}$, where smaller $\alpha$ increases client heterogeneity. The new CARAT results use seeds $42$--$46$; compatible legacy rows use five seeds where available, while FLTrust uses three. Each cell reports the mean and sample standard deviation of final-round test accuracy. Average rank is computed across the four attacks separately for each heterogeneity setting. We do not compare final loss across defenses because the legacy logs and new exporter use different loss normalizations.

CARAT ranks second under ALIE and FangAttack and third under MinMax and MinSum in both heterogeneity settings, giving average rank $2.5$. Its mean accuracy is within $0.11$--$1.22$ percentage points of the best row in all eight cells. It also exceeds MultiKrum and FLTrust in every cell, by $0.9$--$14.1$ and $7.7$--$12.9$ percentage points, respectively, although the FLTrust comparison is qualified by unequal server-reference budgets. Simple Mean obtains the best average rank ($1.25$), and FLDetector narrowly leads on $\alpha=1.0$ MinMax and $\alpha=0.5$ MinSum. Thus, the rerun supports competitiveness and consistency, but not universal superiority of CARAT.

Full CARAT has the highest four-attack mean, but its advantage over the reduced variants is only $0.06$--$0.28$ percentage points, smaller than the seed-level standard deviations in individual cells. Reduced variants also lead on MinMax and MinSum. The study therefore suggests a modest combined benefit, chiefly under ALIE and FangAttack, but does not establish that every component is independently necessary. In particular, $T=1$ nearly matches $T=8$ in this setting, so stronger evidence is needed before attributing the result to repeated hidden tasks alone.

\subsection{Limitations and Diagnostics}
\label{sec:limitations-diagnostics}

The evidence is limited to CIFAR-100/ResNet18, $20$ fully participating clients, a known $20\%$ attacker fraction used by CARAT's capped-simplex prior, and four untargeted model-poisoning attacks. Mean is the strongest method on average in this protocol, so the experiments do not show that CARAT is needed whenever unprotected averaging already remains stable. The reference examples overlap the population from which client data are partitioned, and CARAT and FLTrust use unequal reference budgets. The ablation covers only $\alpha=0.5$ and does not sharply separate the components. Broader datasets, disjoint server data, matched auxiliary-data budgets, larger client populations, stronger heterogeneity, backdoor and data-poisoning attacks, and adaptive attackers that infer the hidden tasks are therefore important next tests.
